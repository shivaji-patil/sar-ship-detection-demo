Maritime domain awareness prototype · Shivaji Patil

Ships in Sentinel-1 radar, checked against AIS

Free satellite radar sees most coastlines every few days, through cloud and darkness. This service is built to find the ships in each new image, check every one against the AIS position broadcasts ships send, and flag the ones no broadcast accounts for, by itself after each satellite pass. Its first live pass was Vancouver harbour on 22 October 2026 (below); it was restarted on 5 November 2026 after its timers were lost.

Scored the way an operator would use it: probability of detection at a fixed false-alarm rate, on 12 passes over US waters where a full year of AIS is public.

In 30 seconds

  • Tested the way an operator would need. Five fresh test sets in 23 new US areas, and a sixth in seven Norwegian and UK production areas: the areas, the pass rule and the success criteria were committed before any data was fetched and pushed to GitHub before scoring (the last three before fetching too), each on a branch of its own; GitHub's push log for each records when it arrived (fresh-prereg, fresh2-prereg, fresh3-prereg, fresh4-prereg, fresh5-prereg, fresh6-prereg). Each was scored once. Results are reported whichever way they came out.
  • A learned detector was not good enough, and the gate gained a rule. A Sentinel-1 ResNet-50 classifier passed the promotion gate, but on fresh water it produced 59% more false alarms than classical constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) detection set to find as many ships. It was not promoted. The gate gained an equal-recall rule, but the classifier still passes it on the original test passes; only the fresh test caught the problem.
  • A false-alarm budget did not travel to structure-dense water. A CFAR threshold set to give 10 false alarms per 1,000 km² on 129 passes gave 21 in five new areas (23 as first scored, before the federal platform list was applied); without Mobile Bay it was 10.1. Most of the excess sits on returns that recur from pass to pass: in Mobile Bay, 87% of false alarms fell on 32 recurring spots, only 4 of them on the federal platform list.
  • The fix held up where structures are unlisted. A map of recurring unexplained returns, built from each region's own first six months, cut false alarms on the dark list by 34% beyond the federal platform list in the next six months of five new areas, and raised Pd at 5 false alarms per 1,000 km² by +0.05 (+0.03 to +0.09), without hiding any AIS-tracked ship. The pre-registered scoring, which left that list out by mistake, gave 41% and +0.08. In five dense federal platform fields (the fifth test), where nearly every structure was already listed, it showed no demonstrated improvement (9% fewer false alarms).
  • Hiding next to a structure. Masking recurring returns has a cost: in Mobile Bay 7 of 37 AIS ships sat at a map spot, because vessels moor at some of them. Spots with an AIS ship beside them are now left unmasked. A flag for structures whose return changes caught 60% of vessels injected beside them in development. In a fourth pre-registered test on five structure-rich new areas, the map with that rule was an improvement (false alarms cut 65%, 2 of 115 AIS vessels at a spot); the flag does not transfer: it caught 66% of injected vessels but added 1.9 false alarms per 1,000 km², over its bar of 1.0, so it stays out of production. A version calibrated on each area's own history, tested in a fifth pre-registered test on five dense platform fields, does not work either (9% caught).
  • On Canadian water. Over the Grand Banks oil fields, with no AIS and no list of structures, the same map found 3 of the regulator's 4 production installations (the pre-registered bar was all four) and the new West White Rose platform, with no other spot. The fourth, SeaRose, appears on every pass 2.0 km from its listed position, and swings too far for the map's 100 m rule.
  • A wider radius for swinging production ships. In a sixth pre-registered test on seven Norwegian and UK production areas, scored against the regulators' installation lists with no AIS, a 300 m map radius found 15 of 16 turret- and buoy-moored production ships against 12 for production's 100 m, but 3 more fell short of the 5 the test needed, and it added 3 places away from any installation. Reading: no demonstrated improvement, so production keeps 100 m.
  • Traceable. Every result on this page is read from a results file in the repository; only the list of scheduled live passes comes from a local file. All but the Danish figures (copied from the Danish-waters study, a separate, private repository) are computed there by scripts; the learned-detector results also need trained weights that are not included. The code has 192 tests, and every detector version and gate decision is recorded in an MLflow registry (exported to results/mlops.json).

How it is built to run

  1. Plan passesPredict Sentinel-1 overpasses of each harbour from the 12-day orbit repeat.
  2. Record AISRecord live AIS from 75 min before to 75 min after each pass (systemd timer).
  3. Wait for scenePoll the Planetary Computer STAC catalogue until the scene is published.
  4. IngestRead only the harbour window of the terrain-corrected Sentinel-1 scene (10 m).
  5. Open-water maskOpen water from Overture/OSM, minus piers and bridges, and a coastal buffer.
  6. CFAR detectorTwo-parameter constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) detection on cross-pol backscatter; fragments within 100 m merged.
  7. AIS correlationAIS interpolated to the overpass; match region stretched along the track by each ship's expected azimuth shift.
  8. CharacteriseLength and axis from the blob, polarisation ratio, platforms flagged (BOEM).
  9. ProductsGeoJSON, KML, image chips, alerts for dark targets in protected areas.
  10. Analyst consoleAccept or reject each target; decisions saved as labels.

Position error

A moving ship appears displaced along the satellite track by about 115 s times its speed toward the radar. So stationary and moving ships are reported separately, and a detection matches an AIS ship only within 300 m of a region stretched along the track by that shift, not in a wide circle. Random detections on the same water would reach Pd 0.04 (the Pd by chance column).

What the coastal buffer changes

With Harbour Twin's original 40 m coastal buffer, most unmatched targets in Puget Sound were on the shoreline: private docks and shore returns that the map does not show (350 unmatched there, against 32 at 150 m). Pooled, the false-alarm rate at k = 5 falls from 48.7 to 13.1 per 1,000 km², and Pd at 5 FA rises from 0.54 to 0.63. The cost is that ships within 150 m of the coast are no longer tested (272 AIS ships scored at 40 m, 226 at 150 m). A detector for berths and marinas needs a better land and structure mask, not a bigger buffer.

The 150 m was picked after looking at these 12 passes, so the figures above are not independent of that choice. As a check, the buffer was chosen again on the 23 separate 2024 training passes alone (best Pd at 5 FA among 40, 75, 100, 150, 200, 300 m) and scored once on the 12 test passes. That picks 300 m, which on the test passes gives Pd at 5 FA 0.66 and 10.6 false alarms per 1,000 km² (150 m: 0.63 and 13.1), but scores only 201 of the 226 ships. So choosing 150 m on the test passes did not flatter the figures above. It also shows that Pd at a fixed false-alarm rate cannot choose the buffer on its own: Pd keeps rising as the buffer widens, partly because the ships near the coast, the hardest ones, leave the test. That is why the promotion gate below also requires nearly all ships to stay scored, and rejects 300 m.

Locked test: three areas never used to choose a production detector setting

The 12 test passes above helped choose the coastal buffer, so they are not a clean held-out set. A second set was therefore fixed before any of its data was fetched: Massachusetts Bay (Boston approaches), the Chesapeake Bay entrance (Cape Henry approaches) and New York Lower Bay (Ambrose approaches), the first Sentinel-1 scene each month of 2024 that covers the area (36 passes, 284 AIS ships, 20,027 km² of open water). The scene list and the detector settings were committed first; the detector is the production CFAR, unchanged. The set was fixed in local commit 2f818e9 (24 Oct 2026) and first pushed to GitHub on 3 Nov together with its scoring, so its order rests on the commit dates (the fresh tests below were pushed before scoring). Large ships hold up on the locked set: Pd for ships of 50 m and more is 0.94 on the locked set against 0.95 on the test set, at the same false-alarm rate. Overall Pd is lower (0.69) because more of its AIS ships are boats under 25 m (about 28% against 21%), and fewer of those are found (about 0.20 against 0.36). Length is the weakest KPI: blobs run long (bias 34 m on the locked set). In the second study, learned length estimators did better (mean absolute error 19–27 m against 44 m for the CFAR blob, on Danish chips).

Position uncertainty. Every detection now carries a 90% ellipse aligned with the satellite track, 473 m along and 138 m across, fitted on 339 matched ships in the training passes. The long axis allows for the azimuth shift of a moving ship (equivalent to 8 kn toward the radar). On passes it was not fitted on, it contains 90% of AIS positions on the locked set.

Scored twice, same detector. The first scoring of the locked set exposed a fault in the truth, not the detector: AIS had been extracted only inside each area's box, so a ship that had just left the box stayed in the truth at its last fix and counted as a miss. AIS is now extracted 0.1° beyond the box; the truth was rebuilt for every set and every result re-run, with no detector setting changed. The first scoring gave Pd 0.58 at k = 5 on the locked set (342 ships, including the stale ones); it is kept alongside this one.

How many unmatched targets are vessels? A stratified random sample of 128 of the 402 unmatched targets on the test and locked passes was labelled from the radar chips alone, without AIS, by Claude, an AI model rather than a trained analyst. Of those judged either way, about 64% look like vessels (55%–73%); 47 were too small or faint to call, and counting all of those either way puts the share between 38% and 91%. So the false-alarm rate likely includes many real vessels without AIS. A trained analyst's review would firm this up.

Truth is AIS: vessels without it count as false alarms, so the false-alarm rate is an upper bound. Breakdowns by area, wind, wave height and incidence angle (nearest NOAA buoy within 60 km) are in the repository's KPI report. The hull axis has a 180° ambiguity: the radar gives orientation, not direction of travel.

A learned detector against CFAR

A two-stage detector: constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) detection at a low threshold proposes candidates, and a small CNN trained from scratch (no pretrained weights) scores each one from a 480 m chip of cross-pol, co-pol and the water mask. It was trained on 20 other 2024 passes over the same areas, each at least a month from any test pass, with AIS matches as labels (324 ships among 9,264 candidates). Model choice and the operating threshold used 3 further held-out passes; the 12 test passes played no part in training, model choice or the threshold, and are scored exactly like CFAR.

Where false alarms are expensive, the learned detector finds about 50% more ships than CFAR on average (Pd 0.32 ± 0.08 against 0.22 at 1 FA), though the margin varies a lot between training seeds. At looser budgets the two are level, and both are capped by the candidates. At production's own false-alarm rate (13.1 per 1,000 km²) they find the same share of ships (0.81 ± 0.01 against 0.81), and the same share of boats under 25 m (0.37 ± 0.03 against 0.36): no gain on small craft. Through the promotion gate it was rejected: its operating threshold, set on the held-out passes to match production CFAR's false-alarm rate there, gave 15.8 false alarms per 1,000 km² on the test passes against production's 13.1. CFAR stays in production; the learned model is registered as version 5 for the next round. The labels are AIS, so vessels without AIS count as false alarms in training as in scoring; 324 positive examples is a small training set.

A second learned detector, and the pre-registered test it failed

Round two keeps the two-stage design but changes the network and its labels. CFAR proposes candidates at k = 4; a ResNet-50 pre-trained on Sentinel-1 (SSL4EO-S12 SoftCon), fine-tuned on co-pol and cross-pol chips, decides ship or not; five seeds are averaged (registry version 6). It was trained on 14 and tuned on 8 development passes from 2024 in three other areas (San Francisco Bay, New York–New Jersey, Hampton Roads), split by date.

What CFAR's false alarms are (294 on the development passes, sorted by rule and checked on chips): about 40% are bright compact targets without AIS, probably vessels; 27% recur on training passes (fixed objects); 19% are shore returns; 10% weak clutter; ambiguities about 1%. The classifier learns AIS-matched ships against the recurring, shore, clutter and ambiguity candidates; the probable vessels without AIS are left out. These are pseudo-labels: a recurring return can be a boat on a regular mooring.

Development comparison. On the 8 development validation passes, at its frozen threshold, it finds 0.72 of the ships at 10.8 false alarms per 1,000 km², against 0.63 for production CFAR at its best threshold under 13 and 0.64 for a dual-polarisation YOLO trained on Danish AIS (paired differences +0.095, interval +0.032 to +0.171; +0.079, +0.017 to +0.153). Can an operator trust a false-alarm budget?

CFAR is meant to hold a constant false-alarm rate, but on the 129 passes used so far its rate at k = 5 varies twenty-fold between areas. Two ways to hold a budget of 10 false alarms per 1,000 km² were fitted on those passes and frozen: one fixed threshold (k = 6), and a threshold set per scene from the scene's own clutter (no AIS needed). With hindsight, per-scene thresholds could reach Pd 0.81 at the budget against 0.75 for a fixed one, so there is room to gain.

Both were scored once on 60 pre-registered passes in five new areas (254 AIS ships; commit fdc8a64 on branch fresh2-prereg, push log):

Correction, found after publishing. This test was scored without production's platform exclusion: its scorer assumed none of the five areas has federal (BOEM) platforms, but 7 stand in the Mobile Bay box. With them left out, as production does, the rate at the fixed threshold is 20.6 (19.0 to 22.2) and 50 in Mobile Bay, so both readings stand. Of Mobile Bay's 32 recurring false-alarm spots, 4 are BOEM platforms, 7 more are offshore platforms mapped only in OpenStreetMap, and 21 are neither.

On the Grand Banks: does the map find the regulator's installations?

A check on Canadian water, where no historical AIS is available. Production CFAR ran on 12 Sentinel-1 passes (HH/HV, November 2025 to October 2026) over the Jeanne d'Arc Basin fields, about 350 km east-southeast of St. John's. The six November–April passes built the map with no list of structures. The installation list (the C-NLOPB layer, plus the West White Rose platform from the provincial layer), the pass rule and the success criterion were committed before any radar data was fetched (local commit d756a57, not pushed before scoring; Tests 1 to 6 were pushed to GitHub before scoring). On the six later passes, 27% of CFAR detections fall on a spot, which is what the map would take off a dark list.

Why SeaRose was missed (looked at afterwards). It is there: a 238 m return appears on 12 of 12 passes, 61 m from Wikipedia's position for the field and 209 m from the World Ports Directory's, but 1,999 m from the regulator's point, which agrees on longitude and differs by about 1′05″ of latitude. It also weathervanes on its turret: its return moves up to 382 m between passes, more than the map's 100 m recurrence radius. Rebuilt with a 300 m radius (about one hull length), the map puts a spot 40 m from SeaRose and none away from the installations, but that radius was chosen after seeing this data, so it is not a result here: it was put to a test of its own on new water (the sixth pre-registered test, no demonstrated improvement). Two lessons: size the recurrence radius to the largest swing (a moored FPSO), and treat a published position as something to check.

A vessel hiding next to a structure

The map takes returns at known structures off the dark list, and production leaves returns within 150 m of a listed platform out. Both would hide a dark vessel moored alongside. Two things were checked on areas already used (development, not a test).

Map spots where vessels sit. In Mobile Bay, which was not one of the structure-map test areas, 7 of 37 AIS ships on the July–December passes lay within 150 m of a spot: some recurring returns are moorings and waiting berths, not structures. Sixth pre-registered test: a wider radius for swinging production ships

Rules, areas, unit list and pass list fixed 6 Nov 2026, 00:07 UTC (5 Nov, 20:37 NST) and pushed to GitHub (commit 79a443c on branch fresh6-prereg, push log) before any of the radar was fetched. Scored once, 6 Nov 2026, 02:32 UTC (5 Nov, 23:02 NST) (commit 5388472).

Second study: Danish waters at scale

A larger study, built in October 2026 in a separate, private repository, uses the Danish Maritime Authority's AIS: 72 Sentinel-1 frames over the Danish straits, 62 over the Danish North Sea, and 94 frames from May 2026 held out until every setting was frozen. Intervals are 95% bootstraps that resample whole Sentinel-1 passes.

Matching that follows the radar physics

Correcting for the shift cuts the median SAR–AIS offset from about 207 m to 65 m on ascending passes and from 200 m to 60 m on descending ones. The match gate in the US validation above uses the same geometry.

Bright fixed structures next to a ship can steal its AIS match. In an injection test with known truth, a cost penalty for detections at mapped structures and recurring sites cut stolen matches from 20% to 3%, with no loss of recall.

Detect on cross-polarisation

Across 70 autumn frames, detecting on VH instead of VV halves the unmatched list, from 27.1 to 13.6 per 1,000 km² (difference -13.5, interval -20.9 to -7.0), at similar recall (80.4% against 79.8%), counted before any static-structure filter. On the windiest winter passes (median wind 10 m/s) the gap is larger: VH 13.5 against VV 63.4 per 1,000 km². Sea clutter is far weaker in cross-pol than ship returns.

How a change reaches the live service

Every validation run is tracked in MLflow with the detector settings, the operating metrics, the results files and the git commit. Each detector configuration is registered as a version of one model, loadable and runnable from the registry. A version becomes production only through a gate, or through an override recorded in the registry (version 2, below). The gate: Pd at 5 FA may not drop by more than 0.02, false alarms may not rise by more than 10%, at least 95% of the ships must still be scored (a wider mask must not buy Pd by testing less), and Pd by chance must stay under 0.06. Promotion writes the production settings to a file the live service reads, and every processed pass records the version that produced it. The decisions so far, replayed through the gate:

Limits

  • AIS is incomplete truth. Vessels without AIS make some "false alarms" real ships, and AIS position errors and fix gaps can break a true match.
  • Small craft. At 10 m pixels, boats under 25 m are often missed. That is a sensor limit as much as a detector one.
  • Near the coast. Ships within 150 m of the mapped coast (berths, marinas) are outside the scored area.
  • Sample size. 12 test passes (226 ships) and 36 locked passes give useful but wide uncertainty, especially in the Gulf (25 ships). All are US waters in 2024, in VV+VH; HH/HV over sea ice is untested.
  • Baseline detector. Production detection numbers are for CFAR. Two learned detectors are reported above, and neither was promoted: one failed the gate, the other a pre-registered fresh test.

Next steps

Done so far (phase 1): operator KPIs with intervals, a locked test set, a position ellipse on every detection, and breakdowns by sea state and incidence angle. Planned:

Phase 2, operational capability. AIS mismatch flags (an AIS position with no radar return while an unexplained target sits within plausible range); heading from the wake to resolve the 180° ambiguity; length and vessel type for unmatched targets; linking unmatched contacts across passes, as the Danish study does; wind-adaptive thresholds.

Phase 3, deployability. Near-real-time Sentinel-1 with calibration in the pipeline, aiming at under an hour from sensing to alert; standard track and overlay formats alongside GeoJSON and KML; an offline container with a software bill of materials, signed images and an audit log; RADARSAT Constellation Mission ingestion, subject to access terms.

Further detector work:

  1. Learned detector, round three. More AIS-labelled passes, analyst decisions from the console as cleaner labels, and a full-image detector so recall is not capped by the CFAR candidates.
  2. Discrimination. Azimuth ambiguities and sidelobes; iceberg-versus-ship with labelled icebergs; analyst review of the structure map's spots.
